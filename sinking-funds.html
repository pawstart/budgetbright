Sinking Funds Explained: Never Get Surprised by a Bill Again

This is general education, not personal financial advice.

A sinking fund is money you save a little at a time for a bill you know is coming, but that does not arrive every month. Instead of paying a large amount all at once, you spread it out in advance. That is the whole idea.

What counts as a sinking fund?

Examples include: car servicing and repairs, annual insurance or subscriptions, Christmas and birthdays, home repairs, school costs, and holidays. The key point is that the cost is predictable in type, even if the exact amount varies.

Costs that are truly unexpected — a sudden breakdown you could not foresee — are what an emergency fund is for. See How to Build a $1,000 Emergency Fund.

How to calculate a monthly amount

Take the expected yearly cost and divide by 12. That gives a monthly saving amount for that fund.

Illustrative example only: if a yearly cost is $600, then $600 ÷ 12 = $50 a month. If a yearly cost is $240, then $240 ÷ 12 = $20 a month. Use your own expected costs and your own numbers — these examples are arithmetic only, not estimates of what anyone actually spends.

If you do not know the exact yearly cost, use a recent bill as a starting point and adjust when you have a better figure.

How to set up separate pots

Give each sinking fund its own pot, envelope, or labelled account, if your bank allows it. Keeping funds separate stops one pot from quietly being spent on something else. A simple list helps: fund name, expected yearly cost, monthly amount, and current balance.

Start with one or two funds, not ten. Car costs and Christmas are common starting points because they are regular and easy to predict in outline.

Sinking funds vs. emergency fund — different jobs

  • Sinking fund: for a known, future cost. You save in advance, then spend the pot when the bill arrives.
  • Emergency fund: for an unknown, urgent cost. You save in advance, but you do not know when, or for what, you will need it.

Keeping them separate stops a predictable bill from wiping out your emergency cushion.

Starter checklist

  • List 2–3 predictable yearly costs.
  • Work out a monthly amount for each (yearly cost ÷ 12).
  • Set up one pot per fund.
  • Move the monthly amounts on payday, if possible.
  • Review the pots in your Monthly Money Reset.

FAQ

What if I cannot fund every sinking fund at once? Start with the one that is due soonest, or the one that would hurt most if unpaid. Add more funds over time.

Can a sinking fund be for fun, like a holiday? Yes. A sinking fund is a saving method, not a judgement about what you save for.

What if the bill is larger than the pot? Pay what you can from the pot and cover the rest from that month's budget. Then adjust the monthly amount for next year based on the real bill.
